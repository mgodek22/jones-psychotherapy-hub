Why High-Functioning Anxiety Is So Easy to Miss

High-functioning anxiety hides precisely because it looks like competence: you keep delivering, so no one — sometimes not even you — sees the strain underneath.

By Emily M. Jones, LCSW, CGP · Jones Psychotherapy, Arlington & McLean, VA

If you're in crisis: this article is educational and isn't a substitute for care. If you're thinking about harming yourself or are in danger, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), text HOME to 741741, or call 911. See the Resources page for more.

What it looks like

It rarely looks like panic. It looks like over-preparing, over-giving, and a mind that won't stop scanning for what could go wrong. It looks like being the reliable one while quietly exhausted, and a quiet voice that says whatever you did wasn't enough.

Because the results are good, the cost stays invisible — until sleep, relationships, or your ability to actually enjoy things start to fray.

“Perfectionism is not the same thing as striving to be your best.”

— Brené Brown, The Gifts of Imperfection

Why it's easy to miss

Anxiety that drives achievement gets rewarded, so it's easy to mistake for a personality trait or a strong work ethic. Many high-achieving women, in particular, learned early that staying ahead kept them safe or valued.

What actually helps

The goal isn't to do more — it's usually to do a little less, and to change your relationship with the inner critic. Approaches like IFS and EMDR get underneath the pattern rather than just managing symptoms. Small, concrete steps help too — you can start with my free guide, Quiet the Inner Critic.

The perfectionism connection

High-functioning anxiety and perfectionism often travel together. Perfectionism promises safety — if I get it all right, I can avoid criticism or shame — but the bar keeps moving, so the relief never quite arrives.

Untangling the two is much of the work: learning that your worth isn't a performance, and that loosening your grip doesn't mean everything falls apart. Anxiety disorders are also the most common mental-health concern in the U.S. and among the most treatable — you are far from alone in this, and it responds well to help.

Small shifts that lower the pressure

  • Swap "I have to" for "I'm choosing to" — and notice what you'd actually drop.
  • Set a "good enough" bar before you start a task, and stop there.
  • Schedule rest like it's a meeting, and protect it just as fiercely.
  • Name the inner critic's voice so you can answer it instead of obeying it.
  • Tell one safe person how you actually feel — secrecy feeds the strain.

Common questions

Is high-functioning anxiety a diagnosis?

It's not a formal diagnosis — it's a common way of describing anxiety in people who keep performing well. It's still worth addressing.

Won't slowing down hurt my performance?

Usually the opposite. Anxiety-driven output comes at a cost to sleep, health, and creativity; easing the pressure tends to make good work more sustainable.

When should I reach out?

If it's constant, affecting sleep or relationships, or you're just tired of white-knuckling it, that's reason enough. You don't have to be in crisis to deserve support.
